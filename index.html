BEWÖÖD: pricing four growth bets on one wooden bowtie

A study digest of the NACRA case by Dolansky, Ruaud and Didier (Case Research Journal, 2020). Summary, every exhibit, worked answers, and what to say in class.

Lille, FranceFounded March 2015Decision date: March 2018Flagship: Mr. Divergent, €393 of 4 decisions due by June

The case in one minute

Laury Bonjean founded BEWÖÖD, a small Lille brand of handcrafted, laser-cut wooden fashion accessories. Three years in, it is a recognized name, and its best-seller is a wooden bowtie called Mr. Divergent, sold for €39 including 20% VAT. That price sits at the low end of a market where premium rivals charge €50 to €95.

In March 2018 she has four growth options, all built around Mr. Divergent. Each one needs a different pricing tool. Her real problem: she has always priced by trial and error and gut feel, and she wants a sound method before choosing which options to launch.

The big insightEvery data set in the case says the same thing: Bonjean is underpricing. Demand for Mr. Divergent is price-inelastic, customers will pay about €10 more for a box that costs her €4.10 more, and her "premium, handcrafted, local" story is priced like a budget brand.

Founder and company

  1. Child
    Grows up in Dunkirk (northern French coast). Her father teaches her woodworking; by age 8 she is building furniture for her Barbie dolls. Fills notebooks with drawings.
  2. Uni
    Studies biology at university in Lille, then joins a work-study program in computer graphics at ESDM, a fashion design school.
  3. Spark
    Fashion-designer friend Jean Laument (whose brand was launched through Maisons de Mode, a City of Lille program for young designers) tells her about laser cutting at the Fablab. She does an internship there, learns to laser-cut her drawings in wood, spends 18 months planning, builds prototypes, takes sewing classes.
  4. 2015
    Launches BEWÖÖD in March 2015, aged 27. First creation, the Mr. Divergent wooden bowtie, becomes the best-seller. Early revenue comes from regional trade shows, local events and a pop-up store in Roubaix.
  5. 2016
    Competition in wooden bowties intensifies, though the market stays fragmented.
  6. 2017
    Online store opened (cheap: low inventory storage costs). About 20 partner stores in France and Belgium. Sales mix: 40% own shop and trade shows, 30% online, 30% other retailers.
  7. Mar 2018
    Bonjean weighs four projects. Box set, pop-up and boutique must be decided by June; subscription is a longer-term strategic call she still wants evaluated now.

How products are made

  1. Hand-drawn sketch on paper
  2. Vectorized on a computer
  3. Parts laser-cut (at the Techshop in Lille)
  4. Handcrafted: sanding, polishing, coloring, varnishing, sewing, assembly in her nearby workshop
  5. Photo shoots and advertising ideas

Support staff are scaled up or down with demand. Products are delivered by bicycle.

Brand values and identity

Name: "be wood", meaning be nature, be wild.

Logo: a geometric bear carrying a bird; signals nature and simplicity, deliberately neither masculine nor feminine.

Materials: pear, mahogany, oak, walnut (chosen for color, graphics and mostly weight); only renewable woods; organic cotton fabrics. Wood supplier 15 km away, boards cut to size to limit waste.

Movement: "slow design"; promotes craftspeople and sustainability; part of a reforestation program. 100% designed in Hauts-de-France.

Customers and marketing

Target customers are urban, "chic", community-minded people aged 18 to 40 who want to reconnect with nature through authentic, responsible products. Wood appeals as warm, refined, unique (every grain differs) and not plastic.

Marketing is mostly non-traditional and online: an information-rich website, heavy social media use (Bonjean credits it as essential and cheaper than advertising, but it needs constant fresh content), contests and sweepstakes to collect e-mail addresses, planned workshop visits and live-streamed production, and coverage in the local paper La Voix du Nord.

Products, sales and channels

Style: geometric shapes with floral inspiration; special orders possible. About 70 women's jewelry items (each available in silver, stainless steel or gold plate), fewer men's categories but more unique designs, and a few children's items.

Market and competitors

Men's accessories are growing, led by young, urban, trendy men. Revenue in some categories (watches, bags, shoes) grew by up to 57% between 2009 and 2014. A century ago, adornment signalled power for men; in the 20th century fashion became mostly a women's domain and men stuck to conservative accessories. By 2018 many young men want to reclaim accessories as playful and charming.

The bowtie's story: born in the 18th century with military roots; serious and elegant for most of the 20th century; then turned into the prop of clowns and nerds (Stan Laurel, Pee-wee Herman, Steve Urkel) and overtaken by the necktie. In the mid-2000s designers such as Alber Elbaz and Alexis Mabille made it fashionable again, as a colorful, personal statement piece. Fabric still dominates, but wood, leather and metal are emerging. Bonjean's idea: a wooden bowtie sits between tradition and modernity.

Competition was thin at launch but intensified from 2016 while staying fragmented. Bonjean doesn't need to be the volume leader; she wants to be the innovator. All premium players compete on the same local, handcrafted, slow-design story, yet she deliberately priced at the low end because a high price "felt wrong" to her.

The four projects

1. Father's Day box set due June

Gift box with Mr. Divergent plus two extra interchangeable fabric knots, so the buyer gets three looks. Could be repeated yearly and for other holidays. Packaging is a key part of the offer. Data: unit costs (Ex. 9 and 10) and a survey of 200 representative customers asking at what price the box is too expensive, and at what price it is so cheap they'd doubt the quality (Ex. 11).

2. Pop-up shop due June

Two-week pop-up in downtown Lille in early June (Father's Day), focused on quality and elegance, with about 20 preselected young designers. Fee €399 buys a corner by a big window on a busy pedestrian crossing, plus merchandising, inventory management, sales staff and reporting. She'd feature Mr. Divergent only and thinks she could charge a little more. Data: past price test at €35, €39, €45 over equal periods (Ex. 12).

3. Old Lille boutique due June

A trendy boutique in a fashionable area, targeting men wanting a chic, sporty or romantic look. The owner loves the product and offers to take only a 25% retail margin (half his usual) while keeping the €39 consumer price. He wants a commitment by June.

4. Subscription box strategic

"BEWÖÖD Box for Men": every quarter, a personalized box of three removable knots for the subscriber's Mr. Divergent. Run in-house (not a third party) to own customer data. Price €20 per quarter, only for existing Mr. Divergent owners, first box free with a one-year commitment. Market: 300+ box offers in France, about 500,000 subscribers, average subscription 18 months. Data: per-box costs (Ex. 13).

Constraints Bonjean statesShe can do any or all four and wants each judged on its own merits. Costs like personnel, rent and FabLab are flexible, so allocated fixed costs won't change whichever she picks, and current capacity can absorb the volume. The binding limit is her own time and energy. Her goals: more market presence plus sustainable, profitable growth, without hurting margins.

Q1

How has Bonjean priced so far, and is it right?

What she did: trial and error, anchored on competitors. She chose the low end of the market (€39) to stay accessible, even though her positioning (handcrafted, local, eco, slow design) matches the premium players at €50 to €95. She didn't start from costs or from measured customer value.

Why it's a problem:

  • Price and positioning send mixed signals. Premium story, budget price. The box-set survey shows people start doubting quality when prices look too low.
  • Her own test (Ex. 12) shows demand is inelastic, so raising price raises revenue and profit.
  • The full-cost margin is a thin €9.60 per tie, leaving little cushion for retailer margins or growth spending.
Answer

She uses competition-based, gut-feel pricing at the low end. She should move to value-based pricing: use costs as the floor, competitor prices as reference points, and customer willingness to pay to set the actual price. The tools for this are already in her exhibits: the survey (box set), the price test (pop-up), channel math (boutique) and CLV (subscription).

Q2

What should the Father's Day box set cost?

Method: Van Westendorp price-sensitivity analysis (a two-question version). Turn Exhibit 11 into cumulative curves. "Too expensive" accumulates upward (if your limit is €44, then €47 is also too expensive). "Too cheap" accumulates downward (if you'd doubt quality at €50, you'd also doubt it at €47).

Price"Too expensive" answersCumulative too expensive"Too cheap" answersCumulative too cheapNeither (acceptable)

Reading the chart

The curves cross at about €48.90: the optimal price point, where as many people think it's too cheap as too expensive (about 41% each).

The share finding the price acceptable peaks at €50 (40 of 200 people).

So the sweet spot is €48 to €50. €49 fits, and it echoes the "9" ending of €39.

Price the box at €49. It's the survey's optimal point, earns €30.13 contribution per box (the highest of any unit option), and turns a €39 purchase into a €49 gift. Caveats to mention in class: 200 hypothetical answers from people she thinks are representative, only two of the four Van Westendorp questions, and no volume forecast, so start with a limited run.

Watch the channel conflictThe pop-up and the boutique are both in Lille. A €45 tie downtown and a €39 tie in Old Lille (and online) can annoy customers. Answer

Join the pop-up. Price Mr. Divergent at €45: demand is inelastic, €45 gives the highest revenue and profit in her own test, and break-even is only 13 ties over two weeks of Father's Day traffic. Also sell the €49 box set there; next to a €45 single tie, the box looks like a bargain (anchoring). The window spot and customer contact are bonus brand value.

The cannibalization testEach boutique sale that is genuinely new adds €17.78. Each one that steals a sale she'd have made directly loses €8.13 (€25.90 − €17.78). The deal stays profitable as long as fewer than about 69% of boutique buyers would have bought from her anyway.
17.78 × (1 − c) = 8.13 × c → c ≈ 0.69

If the class reads 25% as a markup on wholesale rather than a margin, she'd get €32.50 / 1.25 = €26.00 and €19.40 contribution. State your assumption.

Answer

Accept, with conditions. It adds €17.78 contribution per new sale, keeps price parity at €39, and puts the brand in a prestigious shop that reaches new male customers, which fits her goal of market presence. But on a full-cost view it barely breaks even (€1.48), and at his normal 50% it would lose money. So: put the 25% margin in writing for a fixed period (say 12 months), agree minimum order sizes, and track whether buyers are new or existing customers.

Q5

Is the subscription box worth it? (CLV)

Method: customer lifetime value. Average subscription: 18 months = 6 quarterly boxes. First box free, so 5 paid. Price €20 incl. VAT = €16.67 net. Cost per box (Ex. 13): knots 3 × 1.30 + packaging 1.00 = €4.90, plus shipping and newsletter 1.50, overhead 0.30, personnel 1.40 = €3.20. Total €8.10. These per-box costs are mostly real cash costs (shipping especially), so we use the full €8.10.

What the number means

€34.73 is the most she could spend to acquire one subscriber and still break even. Targeting existing Mr. Divergent owners through her e-mail list and social channels should keep acquisition cheap.

The free box pays back by box 2. Each subscriber brings €83 of revenue over 18 months, 2.6× a single tie sale.

Strategic value beyond CLV

Recurring revenue, loyalty, owned customer data, a community, and a test bed for new fabrics. Risks: a new business model, fulfilment work every quarter, unknown acquisition cost, and churn once the commitment ends. No discount rate is given; discounting would trim the CLV slightly.

Answer

Yes, launch it as a pilot after the June projects. CLV is positive in every scenario (€17.60 minimum, €34.73 on average), and the strategic upside is large. €20 a quarter looks safe; three knots at about €6.67 each is consistent with how the box set values extra knots. She could test €22 to €25 later.

Answer

Do all four, in sequence. Every option adds contribution because fixed costs don't change and capacity is available. The box set and pop-up are the clearest wins, the boutique is a strategic yes with safeguards, and the subscription is the long-term play. The overarching lesson is to switch from gut-feel, low-end pricing to value-based pricing.

Formulas

Net price = consumer price / 1.2

Contribution = net price − variable cost

Break-even units = fixed cost / unit contribution

Elasticity = %ΔQ / %ΔP (midpoint)

Wholesale = net retail × (1 − retail margin)

CLV = Σ paid-box margin − cost of free box

Common traps

  • Forgetting to remove VAT
  • Using allocated fixed costs for an incremental decision
  • Treating retail margin as a markup on cost
  • Forgetting the free first box still costs €8.10
  • 18 months is 6 quarters, not 18 boxes
